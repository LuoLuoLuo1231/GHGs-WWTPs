Minimising greenhouse gas emissions from small and medium-sized wastewater
treatment plants
Jerzy Mikosz
Cracow University of Technology, Faculty of Environmental Engineering and Energy, Chair
of Environmental Technologies, ul. Warszawska 24, 31-155 Cracow, email:
jmikosz@pk.edu.pl
Abstract
Municipal wastewater treatment plants often use the activated sludge process in single-stage
reactors with nitrification or in two-stage reactors with nitrification and denitrification. Both
processes, if operated under unfavourable environmental conditions, can lead to excessive
production of nitrous oxide (N O), a gas with a high global warming potential (GWP) of about
2
300). In turn, the decomposition of organic pollutants leads to the release of CO and CH into
2 4
the atmosphere. When analysing greenhouse gas emissions from wastewater treatment, it is also
necessary to consider indirect emissions, mainly related to the use of energy from external
sources. Estimating the magnitude of these emissions based on actual measurements is difficult.
This article presents the results of simulation studies on the potential for reducing greenhouse
gas emissions from small and medium-sized wastewater treatment plants with aerobic sludge
digestion (AD) through operational optimisation of existing plants or the application of new
technologies.
Keywords: wastewater treatment; greenhouse gases; nitrous oxide; activated sludge,
multiphase reactors, computer simulation
1 Introduction
Municipal wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are significant producers of greenhouse gases
(GHGs), including carbon dioxide (CO ) and methane (CH ) from the decomposition of organic
2 4
compounds and sludge treatment processes, and nitrous oxide (N O) from biological
2
nitrification and denitrification processes in the multi-phase activated sludge process.
Shahabadi et al. (2009) estimate that total GHG emissions from a WWTP can reach up to 3.8
kgCO eq. per kg of organic compounds, expressed as BOD in the influent wastewater. Among
2 5
these gases, N O plays a special role, both as a gas with a high global warming potential (GWP)
2
of about 300 and as an indicator of disturbance of biological nitrification and denitrification
processes (IPCC, 2006; Domingo-Felez et al., 2019). The amount of GHG emissions can be
estimated using empirical formulae and generalised indices that only approximate the
emissions. For example, Foley et al. (2010) estimate it at about 0.06-0.25 kg N O-N per kg
2
denitrified nitrogen. Similar figures are given by Law et al. (2012) with N O emissions to be
2
between 0.001% and 25% of the nitrogen load in the reactor influent. Haas et al. (2022) provide
a summary of N O emissions for different WWTP types and using different measurement
2

--- Page 2 ---
methods. The values reported there for WWTPs with multiphase reactors in different
configurations range from 0.00012 to 0.0443 kg N O-N per kg nitrogen in the influent. These
2
are significantly lower than the upper limits reported by Folley et al. (2010) and Law et al.
(2012). However, the actual N O emission is difficult to determine accurately due to the
2
difficulty of making measurements under normal operating conditions. Another approach is to
use simple static mathematical models. Such models have been developed by Monteith et al.
(2005), Bridle et al. (2008) and others. However, the most accurate results can be obtained using
dynamic computer simulation with a properly constructed and calibrated model of a given
WWTP. These models describe N O production by heterotrophic denitrification, such as the
2
ASMN model (Hiatt and Grady, 2008), and by autotrophic nitrification and denitrification
processes, such as the models of Ni et al. (2012) and Mampaey et al. (2013). However, it should
be noted that even these models do not fully describe these processes due to difficulties in fully
understanding autotrophic bacterial N O production mechanisms (Mikosz, 2017; Massara et
2
al., 2017). Recently, there has been a great deal of research into the pathways for N O
2
production by autotrophic transformations (e.g. Li et al., 2017; Lang, 2019; Maktabifard et al.,
2022). This gives hope for the development of new mathematical models describing this process
and the emergence of new measurement methods.
While most research has focused on large wastewater treatment plants using anaerobic sludge
stabilisation, very little attention has been paid to GHG emissions from smaller facilities using
aerobic sludge digestion (AD) as a simultaneous or stand-alone process. Smaller WWTPs
typically use simple biological treatment methods, usually in the form of one- or two-stage
activated sludge. Studies conducted to date on GHG emissions from AD systems have tended
to be of a review nature and based on indicator measurements (e.g . Yapıcıoğlu, 2021; Kemmou
et al. 2022). The novelty of the presented research is the use of dynamic computer simulation
to accurately determine the magnitude and structure of GHG emissions from AD processes
under different operating conditions.
The results presented here are limited to two-stage activated sludge systems with pre-
denitrification, such as the modified Ludzack-Ettinger (MLE), which is one of the most
common. Such a system is potentially a source of significant GHG emissions, especially N O,
2
if the nitrification and denitrification processes are disrupted. The results of this study allowed
general conclusions to be drawn about the considerations and limitations of using two-phase
activated sludge technology with AD of sludge in the context of GHG emissions.
2 Methods
2.1 Description of the wastewater treatment plant
The study was carried out using computer simulation methods with the program GPS-X v.7.01.
The subject of the study was a model of a mechanical-biological wastewater treatment plant
with mechanical treatment without primary sedimentation, with a two-phase activated sludge
reactor, a secondary horizontal rectangular clarifier and aerobic digestion of the sludge located
in southern Poland. Sludge digestion was carried out as a simultaneous process in the biological

reactor or separately in a stand-alone aerobic digestion chamber (ADC), depending on the
layout. An external carbon source could be dosed into the reactor to enhance denitrification,
and a coagulant could be dosed into the effluent of the biological reactor for simultaneous
phosphorus precipitation. In addition, the design of the WWTP includes dewatering of the
digested sludge using a centrifuge and transport of the dewatered sludge for disposal. The
technological scheme of the WWTP system is shown in Figure 1.
Fig 1 Technological scheme of the wastewater treatment plant under study
The municipal wastewater flows into the WWTP at 2,000 m3/d and has quantitative and
qualitative characteristics typical of a plant of this size. The population equivalent (p.e.) for the
WWTP is 8333. The WWTP discharges the treated wastewater into a reservoir which,
according to the Polish ordinance on substances particularly harmful to the aquatic environment
(2019), results in a limitation of the total nitrogen concentration in the treated wastewater to 15
g N/m3 and total phosphorus to 2 g P/m3. The most important plant’s operating parameters are
listed in Table 1. The data for the model comes from the WWTP’s operator and the raw

2.2 Model description and assumptions
The study used the mathematical model Mantis3 to describe N O emissions during
2
heterotrophic denitrification and autotrophic nitrification of nitrogen contained in the
wastewater. Denitrification is included in the model as a 4-step process as described by Hiatt
and Grady (2008), and autotrophic nitrification is based on models proposed by Ni et al. (2012)
and Mampaey et al. (2013) with all respective restrictions. The model is integrated, meaning
that it is applied consistently to all objects included in the simulation model, maintaining
continuity and integrity in the description of biological, chemical and physical processes,
including GHG emissions. Following the standard adopted by the GHG Protocol (IPCC, 2006),
GHG emissions in the model include direct emissions (Scope 1), indirect energy emissions
(Scope 2) and other indirect emissions (Scope 3). This methodology is also adopted as the
mandatory way to report GHG emissions in the EU for companies, firms and businesses
(Directive 2022/2464). The model also takes into account negative emissions (offsets) resulting
from the use of renewable fuels, CO capture and their use as a source of inorganic carbon by
2
micro-organisms.
2.3 Organization of the study
The study included a basic operating strategy that is typical for small WWTPs when removal
of nitrogen and phosphorus compounds is required (e.g. a WWTP has a larger capacity or is
located within the boundaries of a larger agglomeration). Under such conditions, it is necessary
to achieve effective biological denitrification, and phosphorus is usually removed by chemical
precipitation. The GHG emissions in scopes 1, 2 and 3 were determined for typical operating
conditions of a treatment plant with stand-alone aerobic sludge stabilization (Layout 1) and for
a hypothetical operating strategy with simultaneous aerobic sludge stabilization (Layout 2). In
subsequent stages of the study, these systems were subjected to a sensitivity analysis with
respect to the most important technological parameters, such as oxygen concentration in the
reactor, influent temperature and availability of organic substrate for the denitrification process.
The level and structure of greenhouse gas emissions were monitored. During the study, the
required technological efficiency of the wastewater treatment process was ensured. The block
diagram of the tests is shown in Figure 2.

4.2 System with simultaneous aerobic digestion (Layout 2)
It was further assumed that the WWTP would continue to operate under similar conditions to
the baseline, but that sludge digestion would take place simultaneously and in a multi-stage
reactor. The SRT is then 25.6 d, which is sufficient to achieve full sludge stabilisation. After
digestion, the sludge is sent to a sludge holding tank and then to a centrifuge for dewatering.

of excess sludge is lower at 235.7 kg DS/d compared to 272.6 kg DS/d for Layout 1.

5 Model sensitivity analysis
Reducing greenhouse gas emissions is not the only factor to consider when choosing an
operational strategy. Also important is the sensitivity of the treatment system to varying
operating conditions such as oxygen concentration in the biological reactor, wastewater
temperature fluctuations and organic substrate availability. These factors were analysed during
sensitivity analysis of the developed model.
5.1 Oxygen concentration in the reactor
The oxygen concentration in the aerobic zone of the reactor was varied from 0 to 2 g O /m3
2
while monitoring the level of GHG emissions (Figure 2). Oxygen concentrations below 0.1 g
O /m3 prevented nitrification and therefore GHG emissions were relatively low. Above this
2
value, nitrification occurred, but the lack of oxygen resulted in accelerated production of N O
2
in the aerobic zone as an intermediate product of incomplete nitrification, as can be clearly seen
in Figure 3. When the oxygen concentration was increased above 0.4-05 g O /m3, the
2
production of N O gradually decreased, with the process occurring faster and to a lower final
2
value for Layout 2. After exceeding the value of 1.7 g O /m3 in both layouts, an increase in the
2
rate of N O production in the anoxic zone due to incomplete denitrification is evident. For both
2
layouts, at oxygen concentrations above 0.3 g O /m3, it was possible to achieve the required
2
technological efficiency for the removal of carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus compounds.

5.2 Process temperature

Temperature has a significant effect on the course of biochemical degradation processes of
pollutants, especially the nitrification process. During the study, the effect of temperature
changes between 6 and 26 ºC on the production of greenhouse gases was analysed for the
layouts studied. For both systems, gas production decreased with increasing temperature, which
was mainly due to increased nitrification and denitrification kinetics and thus reduced N O
2
production at higher temperatures. Over the whole temperature range, the production was
significantly lower for layout 2 with simultaneous sludge digestion (Figure 5). The effect of
temperature was significantly lower for Layout 2.
