1. Introduction
Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are not only crucial for pollution control, but also play an increasingly significant role in
achieving carbon neutrality (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). They have produced 12.1 % global anthropogenic methane (CH4)
emissions and 4.35 % nitrous oxide (N2O) in 2022 (Crippa et al., 2023). The current estimation methods for the greenhouse gas (GHG)

emissions of WWTPs rely on the uniform emission factors (EFs) proposed by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (Bartram
et al., 2019). The uncertainty of the EFs for WWTPs, however, is among the highest in all sectors, e.g., over 90 % for N2O (Song et al.,
2024). Sources of wastewater, treatment process and operation, discharge standards, as well as seasonal and diurnal variations may all
affect the actual GHG emissions at specific plants (Yin et al., 2024). Therefore, the default IPCC EFs could hardly explain the dynamic
spatial-temporal pattern of GHG emissions (Song et al., 2023). For reliable quantification of plant-wide emissions, it is essential to
perform long-term monitoring on all process units, rather than just biological reactors. Nevertheless, only a quarter of the previous
monitoring campaigns have managed to provide this information (Wang et al., 2024).
Moreover, few studies have systematically examined the impact of WWTPs on the atmospheric GHG concentrations in the sur-
roundings, and particularly, comprehensive assessment of the facility-specific contribution is lacking. It is hypothesized that higher
GHG emission rates do not necessarily lead to a greater increase of the GHG levels nearby, as meteorological conditions can also be
crucial. Based on the same rationale, the influence of each facility needs to be clarified as well to re-identify the key GHG-emitting units
of WWTPs for mitigation. Mobile based remoting sensing of downwind atmospheric concentrations has been recently explored as a
complementary method to on-site GHG measurements (Gålfalk et al., 2022; Moore et al., 2023; Samuelsson et al., 2018). With this
approach, it is found that the CH4 emissions of WWTPs in the United States are 1.9 times greater than expected. But till now, the
accuracy of top-down facility-integrated evaluation remains low.
To address the identified research gap, this work applied AERMOD modelling to quantify the effect of all 18 emission units in an
industrial WWTP on the spatial distribution of atmospheric CH4, N2O, and CO2 over a 20 ×20 km area adjacent to the plant. AERMOD
is one of the well-established dispersion models to simulate steady-state near-field (up to 50 km) Gaussian plume concentrations
(Cimorelli et al., 2005; Perry et al., 2025). A wide range of air pollutants have been studied, such as particulate matter (Jahne et al.,
2015; Tartakovsky et al., 2016), sulfur dioxide (Al-Fadhli et al., 2019; Mendoza-Lara et al., 2023), nitrogen oxide (Ekmekçiog˘lu et al.,
2020; Lee et al., 2021), carbon monoxide (Zhang et al., 2016), ammonia (Tadano et al., 2014), volatile organic compounds
(Zavala-Araiza et al., 2014), and odor (Huang and Guo, 2019). It has also been proven effective for either point or area sources
(Thiruvenkatachari et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2021). With regard to GHGs modelling, it has been applied to characterize the release of
CO2 from refinery sites (Karimi et al., 2023; Kose et al., 2021) or port areas (Mousavi et al., 2021), the leakage of CH4 from oil or gas
facilities (Khalaj and Sattler, 2019; Lan et al., 2015), and the dispersion of CH4/CO2 (Elmi et al., 2021; Talaiekhozani et al., 2018) and
N2O (Yaman et al., 2019) from municipal solid waste landfills as well as collection and transport activities. As for the emissions from
WWTPs, most of the studies focus on odorous sulfur-containing substances (Baawain et al., 2017; Dinçer et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021;
Luckert et al., 2023) or volatile organic compounds (Behnami et al., 2019; Wimolrattanasil et al., 2018; Zoroufchi Benis et al., 2016).
So far, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there has been no report on the modelling of GHG concentration change caused by
WWTPs.
To facilitate the first systematic evaluation of WWTP’s contribution on local GHG distribution, a one-year GHG monitoring
campaign, from October 2022 to September 2023, was conducted at a typical industrial WWTP in Shanghai. The collected emission
data, combined with meteorological conditions and terrain information, were fed in AERMOD to generate monthly distribution of GHG
concentrations. A particular emphasis was placed on the five most populated towns in proximity to the plant. More detailed analysis
was performed on the major process units to identify discrepancies between atmospheric concentrations and emissions. Besides, two
extra months of online monitoring data were utilized to validate the reliability of our modelling results.

2. Methodology
2.1. Field site description
The industrial WWTP of interest is located in the southern rural area of Shanghai, close to the Hangzhou Bay. The five most
populated towns within a 20 ×20 km region adjacent to the plant are Caojing (CJ), Huqiao (HQ), Zhelin (ZL), Xinsi (XS), and Fengxin
(FX). Five parallel anoxic/oxic (A/O) process lines, denoted as Lines A to E, are designed to treat industrial wastewater of 34,000 m3
d(cid:0) 1, as well as a negligible amount of domestic wastewater produced at the industrial park. There is a total of 18 emissions units in it,
including 5 points sources (Duct 1–5) for covered facilities and 13 area sources for opened units. More specifically, Duct 1 collects the
exhaust gas from the primary treatment process of domestic wastewater, Duct 2 covers the equalization and anoxic tanks of Lines A to
C, Duct 3 covers the sludge storage and dewatering, Duct 4 covers for the whole secondary treatments of Line D plus the aeration tank
of Line C, and Duct 5 covers the secondary treatments of Line E. The open facilities are composed of two aeration tanks for Lines A and
B (Ox A/B), five secondary settlement tanks for Lines A to E (SST A-E), four dissolved air flotation tanks for Lines A to D (DAF A-D), one
V-form filter (VF) and one biofilter (BF). Detailed position of each emission unit in the WWTP is highlighted in Fig. 1and S1.
2.2. GHG monitoring
A monthly GHG monitoring campaign, spanning from October 2022 to September 2023, was conducted at the total 18 emission
units of the industrial WWTP. For point sources, the off gas was collected directly from ventilation ducts and stored in 200ml
aluminum foil bags. For area sources, a flux chamber method was applied, in which gas samples were collected every 5minutes and
stored in 200ml bags as well. Collected gas samples were analyzed within 48hours by gas chromatography with flame ionization
detector for CH4 / CO2 and electron capture detector for N2O (Agilent 7890B, United States). In addition, two more months of
continuous GHG monitoring was performed from 18th October to 8th November on Duct 5, and from 22nd November to 13th
December on Duct 2 (both in 2023), by high-resolution cavity ring-down spectroscopy analyzer (Picarro G2308, United States).
2.3. Meteorological and terrain data
Ground-level meteorological conditions close to the WWTP were collected on an hourly basis, including temperature, wind di-
rection and speed, pressure, humidity and cloud cover. The AERMET preprocessor was used to compute the boundary layer charac-
teristics for dispersion modeling, such as mixing height and surface heat flux. Terrain information was also incorporated and treated in
the AERMAP preprocessor to take into account the influence of barriers and elevation patterns on wind flow or pollution dispersion.
The spatial resolution of the map was set as 1km in this study, and so a total of 441 points (20×20km) were simulated each time.
2.4. AERMOD simulation
The AERMOD model was initialized with either the constant monthly or time-varying hourly emission rates. The constant monthly
emission profile was composed of all the 18 process units for 12 months, while the time-varying hourly one covered only Duct 2 or 5.
Specifications of different sources were precisely defined, including the inner diameter, base elevation, release height, and exit velocity
for point sources, or X- and Y-dimensions of area sources. A flat, rural site was adopted for AERMOD architecture, and a two-meter
flagpole height was used for receptors. Landscape features were delineated using digital elevation models to take into account the
local variations in elevation and barriers that could affect airflow and pollutant dispersion. This was further refined by a variable grid
resolution, with greater resolution for higher-emission areas around sources. Corrections on the atmospheric stability classes and
pollutant-specific decay rates were also included to enhance model accuracy, as well as the dispersion behavior of changeable at-
mospheric turbulence or in-air chemical reactions. The above approaches ensured that both spatial and temporal variations in
emissions were correctly represented, allowing the better prediction of GHG concentration pattern for a wide range of scenarios.
2.5. Data analysis
The global warming potentials used in the conversion of N2O and CH4 to CO2 equivalent were 273 and 27, respectively, for both
emission rates and atmospheric concentrations. The total emission rates of the WWTP (in kgCO2-eq d(cid:0) 1) were the sum of all 18 point and
area sources. For point sources, the emission rates were basically calculated as the difference between the concentrations of exhaust gas
and background (in ppm), multiplying the flow rates of exhaust gas (in m3 h(cid:0) 1). For area sources, the emission rates were calculated as
the slope of concentration change in flux chamber (in mg m(cid:0) 3 h(cid:0) 1), multiplying the volume of the chamber (in m3), and the area ratio
of the chamber and emission sources. As for meteorological data, the hourly values of temperature, pressure, humidity and cloud cover
were reorganized to quantify their seasonal or diurnal patterns. The distribution of wind speed and direction was plotted as wind rose.
With regard to the prediction of atmospheric GHG concentrations, their monthly distributions over the 20×20km area were first
exported as the baseline, i.e., 441 points per month for one GHG. The average values of the 441 points for the concentration distri-
butions of a particular month were defined as the monthly overall concentrations by the WWTP. The annual distributions of GHG were
calculated as the average values of the 12-month concentrations on each point, rather than the direct annual simulation via AERMOD.
To evaluate the monthly increase of GHG at those five populated towns, their values were calculated as the weighted averages of the
four closest points. The Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the monthly overall concentrations by WWTP, the

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Pattern of GHG emissions and meteorological conditions
A monthly GHG monitoring campaign was conducted from October 2022 to September 2023, except only one measurement taken
in August and September 2023 due to the extreme heat, Fig. 2a. The obtained N2O, CH4 and CO2 emission rates were converted to CO2
equivalent, and compiled to five categories according to their functions, i.e., Duct 1–5, Ox A/B, SST A-E, DAF A-D, and VF+BF. Note
that the wastewater treated in this plant was mainly of fossil fuel nature, the CO2 emission was thus taken into account (Wang et al.,
2024). In fact, the daily CO2 emission rate of the whole WWTP (11837.2±4279.2kgCO2-eq d(cid:0) 1) was over an order of magnitude
greater than N2O (774.8±889.9kgCO2-eq d(cid:0) 1) or CH4 (728.9±285.6kgCO2-eq d(cid:0) 1). No apparent seasonal GHG emission pattern was
observed, as influent flow rates and pollutant concentrations played a more significant role than temperature. It was shown that point
sources (Duct 1–5) contributed 88.0±6.5% N2O, 75.9±13.7% CH4, and 43.3±7.3% CO2 in the total GHG emissions of this
WWTP, while the biggest area sources were Ox A/B (4.8±4.0% of N2O, 21.6±12.2% CH4, and 41.8±10.6% CO2), Fig. S2. The
other facilities produced less than 10% of the emission in total for all three GHGs.
Features of the meteorological conditions required for AERMOD modelling, i.e., temperature, pressure, humidity, wind speed/
direction, and cloud cover, were presented in Figs. 2b-2e and S3-S4. A clear seasonal pattern was seen in temperature (Fig. 2b), with a
maximum value of 28.5±2.6◦C in July and a minimum value of 6.9±5.0◦C in January. Similar characteristics were observed in
specific humidity, reaching the top of 19.7±1.5g kga(cid:0) 1 in July and the bottom of 3.5±1.8g kga(cid:0) 1 in December (Fig. 2d), as the
specific humidity of saturated air increased with its temperature. On the other hand, atmospheric pressure exhibited distinct seasonal
variations, with a peak in December at 102.8±0.4 kPa and a valley in August at 100.4±0.3 kPa (Fig. 2c). It was probably because
warm air was less dense than the cold one, and tended to rise in summer or sink in winter. Meanwhile, the distributions of annual or

3.2. GHG Concentrations at Populated Towns
The modelled annual distributions of the atmospheric concentrations of N2O, CH4, and CO2 in a 20×20km region were shown in
Fig. 3a-3c, with the position of WWTP at the center. The annual distribution was calculated as the average values of 12 monthly
dispersions of GHG concentrations (Fig. S5-S7), and was converted into a logarithmic ppb or ppm scale. Annual simulation was a more
suitable approach to evaluate the impact of GHG concentrations, than the daily or hourly ones, since the seasonal variations in
meteorological conditions were significant. The red dashed curves on the maps pointed out the areas where the increase of atmospheric
GHG concentrations was greater than 10% of the global annual growth rates. They were defined as the average values since 2001,
which were 0.092, 0.664 or 222 ppb yr(cid:0) 1 for N2O, CH4 or CO2 (Lan et al., 2025a, 2025b), and the respective affected areas were 0.37,

emissions rates. Fortunately, none of the five populated towns was located in those highly affected regions, indicating that the overall
impact of this WWTP was moderate on GHG levels in the surroundings. The Pearson correlation coefficients between the monthly
emission rates and the overall concentrations were 0.988 for N2O, 0.943 for CH4, and 0.931 for CO2, respectively, in support of their
predominant roles (Fig. S8). In the meantime, the Pearson correlation coefficients of wind speeds and pressures were generally be-
tween (cid:0) 0.4 to (cid:0) 0.7 (moderate negative), while the values of temperature and humidity were between 0.4 and 0.7 (moderate positive).

The trends of C/E ratios were found to be associated with the respective emission contributions by point or area sources, Fig. 4e.
Higher percentages of point source emissions correspond to larger values of C/E ratios in most cases, but area sources exhibited a
completely opposite trend. Linear regression was used to fit the C/E ratios of point or area sources, showing reasonably good R2 values
of 0.97–0.98. This finding implied that, for a given amount of emission, the resulting atmospheric concentrations might vary from
96.4% to 101.1%, depending on the ratio of point to area emissions. The lowest increase of GHG concentrations was reached under a
half-point-half-area combination. Possible reasons for the contrasting patterns of C/E ratios of point and area sources might be height
differences, which led to a more diluted near-ground GHG concentration for the former. Besides, it was shown that the majority of
meteorological conditions (e.g., humidity, temperature, pressure, and cloud cover) had a stronger correlation with the C/E ratios than
with the emission rates, especially for area sources (0.71–0.79 against 0.48), Fig. 4f. Hence, further analysis was required to under-
stand the small but non-negligible deviation among various process units.
3.4. Validation by continuous monitoring
To evaluate the accuracy of the GHG simulation results in this work, two additional months of continuous monitoring were
conducted on Duct 5 and 2 from October 18th to December 13th, 2023. The resulting hourly averaged emission profile allowed the use
of time-varying input for AERMOD simulations. These two ducts accounted for 65.8% of the total N2O emissions, 29.5% of CH4, and
25.0% of CO2, as well as distinct CH4/CO2 ratios due to the absence or presence of aeration tanks. Hence, they could represent the
main feature of the GHG emissions from this WWTP. The facility-specific emission rates obtained during this monitoring period were
used to simulate the atmospheric GHG concentration changes for Duct 5 in October and November, and for Duct 2 in November and
December. For periods without monitoring data, constant monthly emission rates were employed as a supplementary model input.
Concurrently, simulations using the constant whole-month emission rates were conducted for comparison.
The hourly and daily output of the time-varying and constant simulations were presented in Fig. 5and S15. The total concentration
of each GHG over the 20×20km area was added up for comparison. It was noticed that the concentrations in “constant” (non-
monitoring) periods were always identical for constant and time-varying simulations, no matter whether the emission input was in a
“constant / time-varying” or “time-varying / constant” configuration. As for the monitoring period, the patterns of reference simu-
lations with a constant emission input were similar among various GHGs or ducts, which was supposed to be determined by meteo-
rological conditions only. The deviation of the time-varying simulations from the constant ones was most evident for N2O, and was
comparable between two ducts. The normalized root-mean-square deviations (NRMSD) of the hourly concentration prediction of N2O,
CH4 and CO2 were 1.67, 0.21 and 0.18 for Duct 2, and were 2.16, 0.31, and 0.57 for Duct 5, respectively. The magnitude of NRMSD was
mostly affected by the coefficient of variation (CV) of emission series (Fig. S16), and the corresponding values of Ducts 2 and 5 were
1.97/1.84 for N2O, 0.32/0.61 for CH4, and 0.16/0.22 for CO2. The NRMSD of the daily concentration prediction decreased by
30–50%, but followed the same trend as the hourly results. Despite the short-term fluctuations, the overall distribution of GHG
concentrations by time-varying simulations matched well with the constant ones (Figs. 5c, 5f and 5i), with R2 values over 0.99 for CH4
and CO2, and 0.86 for N2O. These findings confirmed the validity of using constant monthly emission rates of as the input for AERMOD
modeling, particularly when the variations of influent flow rate/quality and emissions were not high, which could ease the labor
burden of long-term GHG monitoring campaigns.
3.5. Future perspectives
The AERMOD simulation results in this work confirmed that the increase of local GHG levels caused by WWTPs could be
remarkably different from their emission profiles, especially for certain areas of interest. Likewise, the exact contribution of a process
unit on the atmospheric concentration must be taken into consideration for the determination of key GHG-emitting units in WWTPs.
For example, the EFs of CH4 and N2O for the industrial WWTP in this study were 1.17×10(cid:0) 3 kgCH4 kgC(cid:0) O1 Dand 1.07×10(cid:0) 3 kgN2O-N
kgT(cid:0) N1, respectively, which was 0.59% and 6.67% of the default EFs recommended by IPCC. However, the increases of GHG con-
centrations at the five populated towns ranged from 3.1–24 ppt for N2O and 56–378.8 ppt for CH4, corresponding to 1.11% and 2.51%
of global growth rates. More importantly, the C/E ratios of Ox A/B (the area sources of greatest GHG emissions in this study) could be
as high as 2.081–2.167 for N2O and 1.731–1.864 for CH4. As known previously, the hydroxylamine oxidation and nitrifier denitri-
fication pathways in oxidation tank may produce N2O (Song et al., 2024), and the aeration process may promote the release of dis-
solved CH4 to air (Song et al., 2024). Hence, enclosing the open Ox A/B could not only reduce their EFs (Wang et al., 2024), but also
lower down the actual increase of GHG concentrations (turning area sources to point sources). To further verify the observations in this
study, mobile based remoting sensing of GHG around the WWTP would be taken, together with the (isotope) tracer release

4. Conclusions
In short, this work has applied for the first time AERMOD modelling to quantify the effect of all the 18 emission units in an in-
dustrial WWTP on the spatial distribution of atmospheric CH4, N2O, and CO2 concentrations over a 20×20km area adjacent to the
plant. A 12-month GHG monitoring campaign revealed that the overall emission rates of the plant were 774.8±889.9, 728.9±285.6,
and 11837.2±4279.2kgCO2-eq d(cid:0) 1 for N2O, CH4, and CO2. The respective affected areas were 0.37, 8.95 and 4.97km2. The increases
of GHG concentration at the five most populated towns nearby ranged from 13.0 to 83.2 ppbCO2-eq yr(cid:0) 1, which was 1.45% of the global
annual growth rate. Detailed analysis of Duct 1–5 and Ox A/B (contributing a total of 92.8% N2O, 97.5% CH4, and 95.1% CO2
emissions) showed that C/E ratios were consistently smaller than one for point sources, while greater than one for area sources,
regardless of the exact emission. These findings could be used as guidance for the development of GHG mitigation strategies for WWTP
in future.
